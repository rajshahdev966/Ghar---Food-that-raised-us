### What food raised you?

</div>

<br>

For most of us, comfort food was never really about the food.

It was about the person who made it.
The kitchen we grew up around.
The voice that said, *eat before it gets cold.*
The dish that somehow tasted better on the worst days.

**GHAR is an attempt to turn that feeling into something you can visit.**

Not a recipe site. Not a restaurant landing page.
A small, quiet space built around one idea —

> Home doesn't have one cuisine. It has one feeling.

<br>

## 🕯️ The Premise

A bowl of dal-chawal.
A pot of Japanese comfort food.
A plate of nonna's pasta.
A pan of Mexican home cooking.
A bowl of Korean soup, still steaming.
A tagine, slow and patient, in Morocco.

Different hands. Different spices. Different languages for *"come eat."*

**One sentence, underneath all of it:** *someone made this for you.*

That sentence is the whole project.

The first problem was never technical — it was emotional. *How do you make someone feel something with a website?*

The answer became an editorial recipe-book: warm paper tones, handwritten accents, imperfect illustrations, serif type, soft shadows, generous whitespace. Less *application.* More — an old recipe book, left open on a counter.

## 🎨 Frontend as a Creative Medium

Most of my projects start with: *"how do I code this interface?"*

This one started with:

> ### *"How can HTML, CSS, and a little JavaScript make someone feel something?"*

That question shaped everything — spacing, type, motion, pacing.

<div align="center">

| | |
|---|---|
| 🖋️ **Visual Storytelling** | A scroll turned into a narrative arc |
| 🪶 **CSS Craftsmanship** | Atmosphere, texture, and motion — without animation libraries |
| 📐 **Responsive Composition** | Illustration and interaction that hold together at every size |
| 🤍 **Restrained Interaction** | Subtle enough to support the story, never to distract from it |
| ⚡ **Lightweight by Design** | Vanilla JS, CSS animation, no framework weight |

</div>

## 🧵 Built With, Not Built By, AI

GHAR was also a quiet experiment in AI-assisted *creative* development — used for brainstorming, visual direction, artwork references, code, animation, and debugging.

But every decision — what stayed warm, what felt honest, what got cut — still had to come from me.

The real lesson wasn't *"AI can build things."* It was learning to give AI a **visual system and constraints** instead of asking it to *"make something beautiful."* That single shift changed everything about the output.

## 📖 What I Learned

Visual hierarchy. Art direction. Emotional UX. Responsive illustration. CSS animation as language, not decoration. Interaction pacing. Design consistency. AI as a collaborator, not a creator.

Most of all — **creative development is still engineering.** You still need structure, performance, accessibility, maintainability. You're simply solving all of that while also asking one more question:

> *Does it feel right?*
